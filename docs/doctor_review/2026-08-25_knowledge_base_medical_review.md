KB-REVIEW-018
--------------------------------------------------------------
**Category:** Allergy / Substitution safety
**Age group:** All pediatric ages, especially under 2 years
**Type:** NEW KNOWLEDGE
**Topic:** Plant-based "milk" alternatives are not nutritionally equivalent to cow's milk for children

**Current system knowledge, if applicable:** Our system currently has no guidance distinguishing plant-based milk alternatives from cow's milk nutritionally, which matters directly for children with a milk allergy or lactose intolerance who may be given a substitute.

**What trusted evidence says:** A pediatric gastroenterology/nutrition society position paper states that plant-based milk alternatives are nutritionally different from cow's milk and substitution with a product that doesn't provide a similar nutritional profile "can be deleterious to a child's nutritional status, growth, and development." Specific example given: an 8 oz serving of almond milk provides only about 2% of the protein of the equivalent serving of cow's milk; rice milk provides about 8%. Documented adverse effects from misuse of these products in children include failure to gain weight, decreased height, and severe nutrient deficiencies including iron deficiency anemia, rickets (vitamin D deficiency), and scurvy (vitamin C deficiency). The society's position: for the first year of life, only appropriate commercial infant formula should be used as an alternative to human milk (not a plant-based "milk"); beyond the first year, if a dairy-free diet is needed, commercial formula may be preferable to plant-based milk when it's a substantial source of otherwise-missing nutrients (protein, calcium, vitamin D).

**Proposed improvement/addition:** Add this as safety-relevant guidance, specifically to inform any milk-substitution suggestion our system might otherwise make for a milk-allergic or lactose-intolerant child (see also KB-REVIEW-002/003 on milk allergy).

**Why this matters for KidsNutriBite:** This is exactly the kind of substitution our current allergy guidance could otherwise lead a family toward without this context — directly safety-relevant and fills the "food substitutions" gap identified as a priority area.

**Trusted source(s):** North American Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (NASPGHAN) Committee on Nutrition — "Position Paper: Plant-based Milks." *Journal of Pediatric Gastroenterology and Nutrition*, 2020;71:276-281.
**Exact source location:** https://naspghan.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/North_American_Society_for_Pediatric.30.pdf — opened and read directly this session (PDF text extracted), 2026-08-25.
**Doctor question:** Should KidsNutriBite actively warn against plant-based milk substitution for milk-allergic children, or simply present the nutritional comparison neutrally and let the family/clinician decide?

KB-REVIEW-019
--------------------------------------------------------------
**Category:** Allergy
**Age group:** Infants (specifically before 6 months, per this evidence)
**Type:** NEW KNOWLEDGE
**Topic:** Earlier introduction of peanut reduces peanut allergy risk

**Current system knowledge, if applicable:** No current guidance on peanut introduction timing.

**What trusted evidence says:** A peer-reviewed analysis found that earlier introduction of peanut to an infant's diet was associated with improved effectiveness at preventing peanut allergy, and that introduction before 6 months of age was more protective than later introduction. The paper references official guidelines (from the U.S. National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases) recommending early peanut introduction as policy, though this paper itself does not state the exact gram amount/frequency those official guidelines recommend (see KB-REVIEW note on insufficient evidence for that specific figure, below).

**Proposed improvement/addition:** Add general guidance that early (not delayed) introduction of peanut-containing foods is now the evidence-based recommendation, contrary to older advice to delay allergenic foods.

**Why this matters for KidsNutriBite:** This reverses what used to be common advice (delay allergens) and is high-value, frequently-relevant guidance for a system advising on infant feeding.

**Trusted source(s):** Logan K, Bahnson HT, Ylescupidez A, et al. "Early introduction of peanut reduces peanut allergy across risk groups in pooled and causal inference analyses." *Allergy*, 2023 (published online Dec 2022).
**Exact source location:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10202125/ — opened and read directly this session, 2026-08-25.
**Doctor question:** Should KidsNutriBite give general "early introduction is protective" guidance without also giving a specific recommended amount/frequency (which I could not independently verify this session), or should this item wait until that specific figure is properly sourced?

KB-REVIEW-021
--------------------------------------------------------------
**Category:** Condition (anemia / iron deficiency) / Feeding
**Age group:** From 6 months (complementary feeding period), through 24 months
**Type:** EXISTING INFORMATION — COMPLETION
**Topic:** Iron-rich foods during complementary feeding

**Current system knowledge, if applicable:** Our system has some general content about iron and anemia but not a clear statement connecting iron-rich food introduction to the start of complementary feeding specifically.

**What trusted evidence says:** Complementary foods first introduced at around 6 months of age should be iron-rich — specific examples given are meat, meat alternatives, and iron-fortified cereals. The paper notes this period (6–24 months) is when iron requirements per kilogram of body weight are higher than at any other developmental stage, because of rapid growth, making this the period of highest iron-deficiency risk.

**Proposed improvement/addition:** Strengthen/add explicit guidance connecting the 6-month complementary-feeding milestone to a need for iron-rich first foods specifically.

**Why this matters for KidsNutriBite:** Anemia/iron deficiency is already a topic in our system; this adds the specific, actionable "what to feed" guidance tied to the highest-risk age window.

**Trusted source(s):** Miniello VL, Verga MC, Miniello A, Di Mauro C, Diaferio L, Francavilla R. "Complementary Feeding and Iron Status: 'The Unbearable Lightness of Being' Infants." *Nutrients*, 2021.
**Exact source location:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8707490/ — opened and read directly this session, 2026-08-25.
**Doctor question:** Should this be paired with our existing anemia content as a single, clearer entry, or kept separate as feeding-stage guidance?

**Category:** Allergy / Condition
**Age group:** All ages (most relevant in infancy/early childhood)
**Type:** EXISTING INFORMATION — CLARIFICATION
**Topic:** Lactose intolerance being listed as if it were an allergy

**Current system knowledge, if applicable:** Our system currently lists "lactose intolerance" in the same list as true food allergies (milk, nut, egg), with its own severity rating alongside them.

**What trusted evidence says:** Lactose intolerance and milk allergy are different conditions. **Proposed improvement/addition:** Decide whether lactose intolerance should be clearly separated from the true-allergy list or otherwise clearly labeled as a different kind of condition.

**Why this matters for KidsNutriBite:** These call for different practical advice; conflating them could lead to advice more restrictive than necessary.

**Trusted source(s):** NIDDK (NIH) — "Definition & Facts for Lactose Intolerance." Corroborating: Luyt D, et al., BSACI 2014 cow's milk allergy guideline.
**Exact source location:** https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/lactose-intolerance/definition-facts — opened directly, 2026-08-25.
**Doctor question:** Should lactose intolerance remain in the allergies file with a clear "not a true allergy" label, or be moved elsewhere in our knowledge base?
