**3.3 — Egg Allergy and Medication/Vaccine Safety**
- **Current KB:** No record.
- **Verification, split into its two components since they turned out to have very different evidentiary support:**
  - **Yellow fever vaccine:** Confirmed real and clinically nuanced, not a simple blanket contraindication. Yellow fever vaccine (egg-based) carries a genuine contraindication label for patients with a *severe/anaphylactic* egg allergy specifically, but current allergy-society guidance describes a manageable pathway (skin testing, then either standard administration with observation if negative, or graded/incremental dosing under medical supervision if positive) rather than an absolute "never vaccinate" rule.
    - **SOURCE 1:** *Safe administration of yellow fever vaccine in patients with suspected egg allergy.* PMC10509842 (peer-reviewed). Access date: 2026-08-31.
    - **SOURCE 2:** *Vaccination and egg allergy: Considerations for family physicians.* PMC11126281 (peer-reviewed). Access date: 2026-08-31. Both independently describe the same skin-test-then-graded-dose protocol.
  - **Propofol:** **The proposed warning is NOT well-supported by current evidence and should likely be rejected or substantially softened, not approved as stated.** Modern evidence — a British Journal of Anaesthesia analysis, a large Australian retrospective study of 28 egg-allergic children across 43 sedations, and a 2021 review — converges on: propofol's lecithin component is egg-**yolk**-derived, while the great majority of clinically significant egg allergy is to egg-**white** proteins (ovalbumin, ovomucoid, conalbumin); there is **no confirmed mechanistic or strong clinical-outcome link** between egg allergy and propofol reactions beyond a handful of old, unconfirmed case reports; current recommendations state propofol has **no contraindication** for non-anaphylactic egg allergy, and even for a history of egg anaphylaxis, either a trial dose or an alternative agent is a clinical decision, not a blanket avoidance rule.
    - **SOURCE 1:** *No evidence for contraindications to the use of propofol in adults allergic to egg, soy or peanut.* British Journal of Anaesthesia, 2017. URL: https://www.bjanaesthesia.org/article/S0007-0912(17)30526-3/fulltext. Access date: 2026-08-31.
    - **SOURCE 2:** *New perspectives on propofol allergy.* 2021 review, PubMed 34309659, independently reaching the same "no routine avoidance needed" conclusion, alongside the specific pediatric Australian cohort study (*Allergic Reactions to Propofol in Egg-Allergic Children*, PubMed 21467558) showing only 1 reaction in 43 sedations across 28 egg-allergic children. Access date: 2026-08-31.
- **Confidence:** HIGH on both components — but pointing in different directions (yellow fever: real, nuanced, worth a careful note; propofol: outdated caution, not current evidence).
- **Recommendation:** **Correct, but needs modification — reject the proposal as written.** If anything is added at all: a yellow-fever note is defensible (with the "not absolute, needs specialist evaluation" nuance, not a flat avoidance instruction), but the propofol warning should **not** be added — adding it would mean actively introducing outdated, evidence-contradicted content into the KB. On the doctor's own scope question (is a medication/vaccine warning appropriate for a nutrition platform at all): given how far outside "nutrition" this content already sits, and given one whole half of it (propofol) turns out to be actively wrong per current evidence, this reinforces rather than undermines the case for the doctor's proposed "exclude — out of scope" option. Priority: **LOW** (recommend exclusion; if the doctor wants *any* egg-allergy/medical-procedure content in scope at all, it should be limited to the yellow-fever nuance, clearly caveated, and never the propofol claim).
